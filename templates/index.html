03

What the flames told us

Four questions researchers keep asking, answered from the measurements. Nothing on this page is predicted.

Fuels compared Measured

FLEX burned single droplets of two fuels. Heptane behaves like a fuel that hides: its flame went out leaving most of the droplet unburned.

Diluent gases compared Measured

Part of the nitrogen was swapped for carbon dioxide or helium, the gases a fire-suppression system might release. Each bar is how the tests in that group ended.

Groups were run at different oxygen levels and droplet sizes, so differences between bars are not the effect of the gas alone. The Simulator isolates one factor at a time. Xenon is not in this table.

Cool flames: when the fire only looks out Measured

  1. The hot flame diesA large droplet loses heat by radiation faster than the flame makes it. The visible flame goes out while fuel is still there.
  2. A cool flame can take overFLEX found heptane droplets that kept shrinking with no visible flame, burning by low-temperature chemistry. Too cool to glow, and too cool to make soot.
  3. Why it mattersUnburned fuel plus a hidden reaction means a fire that can come back. "No visible flame" is not proof that it is out.

Soot: which fuels smoke first Measured

SPICE lengthened a gas flame until it began to release soot. That length is the smoke point. A short bar means a fuel that soots easily.

FLEX: NASA PSI-69. SPICE: NASA PSI-107, ISS tests only. Cool flames: Nayagam et al., Combustion and Flame 159 (2012). The three explanation steps summarize published findings; the charts are from the tables.

04

Run a fire

Set the conditions. The simulator shows how the flame behaves, flags hidden danger, and suggests what would make it safer.

How the simulator works
  • The flame model is machine learning trained on {{ ml.rate_tests }} FLEX droplet burns. It predicts three things: the chance the hot flame goes out early (ranked correctly {{ '%.0f' % (ml.extinction_auc * 100) }}% of the time on unseen test series), the burning rate (R² {{ '%.2f' % ml.rate_r2 }}), and the fuel left behind (R² {{ '%.2f' % ml.fuel_left_r2 }}).
  • The advice re-runs the model with one factor changed at a time and reports what improves. It only suggests changes inside the tested range.
  • Limits: fuel droplets, not solid materials; no cool-flame temperatures. Smoke is handled separately in the Lung model, because NASA measured droplet flames and smoke in different experiments.

05

What the crew breathes

A physics model that turns NASA's smoke measurements into a dose in each part of the lung.

  1. NASA measured the smokeFor each overheated material, SAME recorded how many particles there are and how big. Smoke is a mix of sizes, mostly 0.05 to 0.5 µm.
  2. Smoke changes as it driftsParticles collide and stick, so there are fewer, larger ones. We fitted that rate to {{ aging.n_pairs }} fresh/aged SAME pairs. Diluted smoke ages slowly.
  3. Every size has a fateThe ICRP 66 lung model gives the chance a particle of each size sticks in each region. Big ones hit the nose and throat; tiny ones wander into the alveoli; those near 0.3 µm are mostly breathed back out.
  4. Gravity shifts itSettling under gravity matters for 0.5 to 3 µm particles. Without it, fewer of those deposit. Reduced gravity is shown as a band, not one number.
